Mara Ellison had kept the light at Gullwick for eleven years, long enough that the island had stopped feeling like a place and started feeling like a schedule. She rose before the gray, boiled water, laid a hand on the generator the way a person greets a difficult neighbor, and climbed. One hundred and eighteen steps. The last three were always colder. From the lantern room the sea showed its whole complaint, black water shouldering granite, a channel where freighters still trusted her beam more than their instruments, and the wreck mark on the chart, penciled there by a keeper who had drawn birds in the margins and labeled none of them.

On the eleventh morning after the supply boat missed its landing, she found a sentence in the station log that she had not written. The hand was hers. The date was three nights ahead. It read: Hold the beam on the northeast spit until the second light answers, then let it go. She closed the book, opened it, and watched the line remain, the ink dry, the pressure of the pen familiar from cold mornings. She made tea and did not drink it. Gulls cut the fog into rags, and the fog stitched itself back together before the rags could fall.

She climbed anyway, because the light did not take an interest in what the book claimed. In the lantern room the great lens turned with a planetary patience, and the beam went out over water the color of a bruised plum. She set her palm on the brass rail until the cold became a fact she could use. Far off, where the chart showed only depth, a point of light winked once and then held. It was not a ship. Ships carry stacks of windows and the long grammar of wakes. This was a single column, narrower than hers, the color of a match struck inside a jar.

The strange beam touched hers. The two columns stood against each other like the halves of a bridge nobody had agreed to fund. Mara eased the mechanism one degree toward the northeast spit, as the sentence had asked, and the other light slid to meet her. For a breath the channel was a white road. Then the stranger beam vanished, as if a hand had closed around it. She went down and wrote beneath the line she had not authored: Second light at wreck bearing. Answered. She disliked the word. Lights did not answer. They warned. She underlined warned until the paper furred.

By afternoon the fog had become a material. The radio coughed and produced the harbor master, tinny and determined to be kind. "We're not coming, Mara. The glass is falling through the floor. You have stores?" She had flour, tins, coffee, and a jar of apricot jam saved for no occasion she could name. She said so. He said her name the way people do when they are trying not to build a promise. "Sit tight." The radio returned to weather, which was honest. She spent the evening on brass that was already bright. Every keeper before her had done the same. Their names lived in the drawer: Pell, Okonkwo, Singh, Voss. Mara had added her own on the first night, small, in case the book refused a loud claim.

The future sentence sat above today's entry, which was impossible and, she was learning, ordinary for Gullwick when the glass fell. Sleep came in shards. She dreamed the stair counted her. She woke to a wind that had organized into long shoulders against the tower, and the lantern room ticked the way a hull ticks when it remembers it was trees. She climbed with a flashlight in her teeth and found the lens already bright. The timer did that. She always set the timer. Even so, the room felt attended, as a kitchen feels attended when someone has just stepped out and the chair still holds a shape.

At two the second beam returned, closer. Under it she could see the suggestion of a rail and a room, a tower standing on water that would not hold a tower. A figure moved in that glow, small as a flaw in the lens. The figure raised an arm. Mara kept both hands on the brass. Downstairs the log had grown a line, still in her fearful rightward lean: If she signals, signal back. The gallery door found a note in the wind and held it. "No," Mara told the kettle, and drank the tea this time, and climbed again, because leaving a light in conversation with nobody was worse manners than answering.

At the rail she worked the shutter in the pattern her manual still printed as a footnote: two long, one short, a code retired before she was born. The figure replied with the same grammar of dark and bright. The footnote said the pattern meant I am here. A second line, in smaller type, said it had also meant I am remaining. Mara lowered her hand and felt the ridiculous urge to apologize to a horizon. The beam swept on, doing its job, indifferent to codes and to the small human need to be met.

The storm reached the rock near four with the indifference of a guest who had been invited by the barometer alone. Rain arrived in sheets. The generator coughed, caught, and coughed again. Mara nursed it with the flat of her hand and a word learned from a city mechanic in a life that had contained streets. The beam stuttered, then steadied. Somewhere in the rain the second light stuttered in sympathy, half a second late, an echo that had decided to become a voice of its own. She went down for oil she did not need, and found the store door unlatched.

On the threshold stood a person in a yellow coat as bright as a caution sign. Young. Soaked through. A duffel in one fist and the other hand raised, empty. A woman, Mara judged, though the hood consumed the evidence. Rain wrote rivers on the coat. "The skiff is on the lee rocks," the woman called. Her voice was hoarse and plain, which helped more than any papers could have. "I aimed for your light. Then another one sat just beside it and I followed the wrong one for a while. I'm sorry. I didn't know there were two."

"There aren't two," Mara said. "All right," the woman said, agreeing because the rain was colder than pride. "I'm Ness. Relief keeper. The service said you go quiet on the radio after dark, so they sent the letter with me." Mara had not gone quiet. She remembered the harbor master's voice, and the careful hole where a promise should have been. She also remembered, with the unpleasant clarity of a person standing in two weathers at once, that memory had become negotiable since the log started writing ahead. She stepped aside. Ness came into the smell of wool, tea, and a kerosene ghost that electricity had never managed to evict.

Ness dripped on the slate and studied the log the way a careful person studies a dog. "They told me the book runs strange at Gullwick. I thought they were hazing me." "You're not posted here," Mara said. "I'm posted here until the sea changes its mind." Ness pushed the hood back. She looked about thirty, with a fresh cut on one cheek and eyes already doing the keeper's arithmetic on the room: exits, heat, the stair. "My orders are pulp. Radio them in the morning. If I'm a lie, I'm a lie who can hold a wrench while your generator auditions for a different career." Above them, the generator missed a stroke. Both women looked at the ceiling with the same irritated affection.

"Shoes by the stove," Mara said. "Kit's in the drawer." She cleaned the cut while Ness talked. A cousin had slipped her the old signal card and a warning not to chase lights absent from the chart. For a minute there had been two charts. "What date is on your orders?" Mara asked. "Thursday." "This is Monday." Ness went quiet. "Then keep the Monday," she said. "Show me the stair and the generator's grudge. If Thursday already exists somewhere, it can arrive by ordinary work. I won't call you finished unless you ask." They climbed as the wind dropped. Ness counted under her breath and stopped when Mara did not need her to perform. In the lantern room the true beam laid its road on the water. A thin false light scratched the wreck bearing and waited. Mara aimed down the channel. "Hold," she said. Ness said it a half-beat late. The scratch went out. At dawn the harbor master called, sick with worry, asking whether a fool named Ness had survived her skiff. Mara pressed the handset. Ness watched, coat drying to the color of butter. "She reached me," Mara said. "The light held."
