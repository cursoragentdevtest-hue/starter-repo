The fog arrived before the wind.

Elias Wren saw it from the gallery of Greywick Light, taking the black rocks one by one until the horizon was a rumour. Seventeen years he had kept the lamp, and he knew the colour. It meant a storm by midnight and a meaner one by morning. He trimmed the wick, set his palm on the brass, and felt the flame's honesty in the heat. The clockwork turned. The beam went out over nothing and came home.

No one climbed the cliff after dark. The spring rains had eaten the road, and the parish was content to let a keeper manage alone. Elias ate bread and cold fish, entered the weather in the log, and wound the mechanism a second time. Once was habit. Twice was a promise.

At nine the wind put a shoulder to the tower and the glass began to sing. Rain struck the dome like gravel. He checked the oil and the bolts and climbed again, counting. One hundred and twelve steps, the same as the night the clerk had given him the keys and said the previous keeper had left in a hurry, and that the light was not allowed to fail.

Hart had signed the old logs. The name was cut into the underside of the gallery rail, and written once in a margin: Hart, M., relieved. No cause. Ordinary nights before that line, then blank pages until Elias began. He had never asked the village. A question was a door.

From the gallery he saw the boat.

A small skiff, riding high, no mast and no figure, sitting just inside the beam. On one pass it was there. On the next it was closer. Then the fog took it.

He went down with the oilskin and a lantern. The cove path was mud. The sea broke white on the shingle, and at last the beam found the skiff beam-on to the rocks. He waded in to his knees, caught the painter, and hauled it clear. Inside lay a coil of rope, a bailer, a woman's wool coat folded as if someone meant to return, and in the pocket a key.

He knew the key. Its twin hung on his ring. It opened the tower, the oil store, and the log cupboard. Standing in the rain, he understood the night had ceased to be the sort he knew how to write down.

The found key turned in the door. He hung the coat, set the key beside his own, and climbed to the lamp before he did anything else. The flame was steady. He wound the weight anyway, and the act settled him.

A woman was sitting in his chair.

Rain in her hair, a fisherman's jersey, her hands around his empty mug.

"You kept it lit," she said.

"The door was bolted."

"The key was in the boat. You brought it up. I dislike swimming the last of it."

"There was no one in the boat."

"There was a coat. That was enough to follow."

He wanted to be afraid and found only the annoyance he kept for parish letters. "Who are you?"

"Mara Hart. My father kept this light. I was born in the room you use for rope."

"Hart left twelve years ago."

"Eleven and ten months. I read your log while you were on the rocks. You do not invent weather you did not see."

"You had no right."

"I had a reason." She turned the mug, though it was empty. "On the night he wrote relieved, my father put the light out."

The tower shuddered in the wind. Elias heard the clockwork still turning, and only then sat down. "Go on."

"Not for long. Long enough. A barque called the Cormorant struck the reef they call the Anvil. Thirty-one aboard, nine ashore. He had been paid to let the dark do the work. My mother was dying in a room he could not afford in town, and he was not as brave as a lighthouse requires. He relit the lamp, wrote the word, and sent me to my aunt without looking back. I looked back. He left because if he had stayed he would have told someone, and the parish would have had to decide what a keeper is for."

"Why come tonight?"

"The owner's son means to pass the Anvil with his lights doused, to prove the reef is a tale old men use to keep strangers off a good line of fish. He does not know the story is a grave. I want the lamp burning if someone pays you to do otherwise. They have tried already, in small ways. A barrel of oil left without an invoice. An unsigned letter that told you to rest the light between two and four, when no decent vessel would be abroad. You burned the letter. I saw you from the cliff, and then I knew you would hear the rest."

"You have been watching the tower."

"The light," she said. "There is a difference. The tower is stone. The light is a decision you make each time you take the crank."

A thin sound rose under the rain, metal on metal, seaward of the Anvil.

"If you are lying, you chose a poor night."

"If I am lying, a lamp left burning still does no harm. That is the mercy of the work. The honest act and the cautious act are the same act."

He nodded and climbed.

In the lantern room he checked the wick, the reservoirs, and the rotation, and set the spare where his hand could find it. The beam swung. At the limit of sight a darker notch moved where the reef's broken water should have been alone. He caught the bell rope and hauled. The cove bell spoke, large enough to find a hull. He hauled until his shoulders burned. The notch hesitated, then sheered off. On the next peal a small light showed aboard, then a second, and the shape turned its shoulder to the Anvil and ran for open water, scudding badly and alive.

He stayed at the glass until the shape was gone. Downstairs the chair was empty. The mug was dry. Both keys lay on the table, and the coat's pocket was empty, and he could not tell which key had come out of the sea.

He wrote in the log. Wind south-west, rising. Heavy rain. Fog. Boat in the cove, unmanned. Bell sounded for a vessel near the Anvil. Vessel stood out. Light burning. He did not write her name.

Near dawn the rain thinned. Fog tore in long strips and showed the reef, black and ordinary, worried by a line of white. No wreckage. No skiff on the shingle. The sea had rearranged what it had lent him. He wound the mechanism again, made tea in the mug she had held, and drank it standing up, because the day had begun whether he sat or not.

In the afternoon the parish clerk came to the broken road and shouted that a trader had complained of a bell in foul weather. Bells, he called, were for wrecks and not for nerves. Elias shouted back that the bell was for the living. The clerk made a note and went away. Elias entered the visit, the complaint, and the fact of the light. He hung the strange coat beside his own and put the second key on the ring.

That night the sky cleared. The beam walked its circle over water that looked, for once, like water, and a ship went by the channel with every lamp lit, keeping a respectful distance from the Anvil, as ships do when they can see the thing that would otherwise take them. Elias stood on the gallery with his hands in his pockets and listened to the clockwork.

In the morning a fresh cutting stood beside her father's name, small and careful. Hart, M. Under it, three words: It holds. He did not sand them away. He touched the letters as he touched the brass, to know a true thing, and went down to record a fair wind from the north and visibility clear to the headland.

He wrote that ships were passing and the lamp was well. When he wound the weight the second time he understood the extra turn had never been for him alone. It was for every hand that had found the crank in the dark, afraid or ashamed or only tired, and had chosen even so to haul the light back up into the weather.

He washed the mug and left it upside down, clean, the way you leave a thing when you are willing to be wrong about whether someone means to come back. The fog stayed off for days. When it returned he met it as he always had, except that the reasons now had names, and the names made his readiness heavier and more entirely his.

He kept the light.
